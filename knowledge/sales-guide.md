# EAC Sales Digging Guide

A practical guide to help Sales Agents uncover customer needs before connecting those needs to a relevant learning journey.

Flow: ASK → LISTEN → DIG DEEPER → UNDERSTAND → CONNECT → RECOMMEND
Principle: ASK → LISTEN → DIG DEEPER — Don't chase every question. Chase an understanding of the customer.

## Point 01 — Why English?: WHY DO YOU WANT TO LEARN ENGLISH?

Need: Find the real reason English feels important to the student/customer. Look for the context, the trigger, and who is pushing the need the most.
Why it matters: Answers like "so they'll be smart" aren't enough to shape a recommendation. More specific reasons help the advisor understand both the practical need and the student's motivation.
Signals to look for: Reasons that recur, urgent needs, parent expectations, student motivation, and situations that often leave the student struggling.
What to avoid: Don't settle for a generic answer. Don't jump straight into explaining the program. Don't steer the answer by offering too many options at once.

### Point 01 (Why English?) — Initial Walk-In

Strategy: Build rapport, observe the customer's and student's reactions, then draw out the answers that surface naturally.
Useful questions:
- "Mom, may I ask what makes English feel important for [name] right now?"
- "If it's about being more confident, in what kinds of situations do you want them to be braver?"
- "And from [name]'s own view, why do they feel English matters?"
Dig deeper: If the customer answers "so they'll be more confident," don't move on yet. Watch the student's expression, then ask for the context: school, presentations, friends, or another situation.

### Point 01 (Why English?) — Initial Phone Call

Strategy: Keep the conversation light, one question at a time, and pick up on tone, hesitation, and the main concern.
Useful questions:
- "May I ask what's driving [name] to learn English right now, Mom?"
- "If the goal is more confidence, which situations need it most?"
- "Is this mostly a school need, a plan for the future, or [name]'s own wish?"
Dig deeper: Take one keyword from the answer. If it's "confidence," follow up briefly: "Confidence in everyday speaking, or when presenting, Mom?"

### Point 01 (Why English?) — Initial Chat

Strategy: Send short messages, 1–2 questions at a time, and use the customer's own words to shape your follow-up.
Useful questions:
- "May I ask what makes English important for [name] right now, Mom?"
- "On confidence — in what moments do they usually want to feel more sure of themselves?"
- "Is the need more about school, future plans, or does [name] genuinely want to improve?"
Dig deeper: Mirror the customer's words. If the customer writes "so they'll be confident," reply with context: "More confident when speaking at school, chatting with friends, or in other situations?"

## Point 02 — Biggest Challenge: WHAT IS YOUR BIGGEST CHALLENGE?

Need: Find what could hold the student back from reaching their goal: confidence, exposure, practice, consistency, schedule, motivation, past learning experiences, or parent concerns.
Why it matters: A goal without understanding the barrier can lead to an unrealistic recommendation. The barrier helps the advisor see what needs attention in the learning journey.
Signals to look for: Recurring problems, past negative experiences, student resistance, parent concerns, and obstacles that could affect consistency.
What to avoid: Don't immediately argue or "fix" the problem. Don't blame the student. Don't make a diagnosis the customer's answers don't support.

### Point 02 (Biggest Challenge) — Initial Walk-In

Strategy: Build rapport, observe the customer's and student's reactions, then draw out the answers that surface naturally.
Useful questions:
- "In your view, what's the part [name] struggles with most right now?"
- "At school or when using English, where do they usually get stuck?"
- "Have they taken a course or learned English somewhere before? How was that experience?"
Dig deeper: If the customer mentions "lacking confidence," look for the trigger situation and past experiences. Don't offer a solution before you understand the root cause.

### Point 02 (Biggest Challenge) — Initial Phone Call

Strategy: Keep the conversation light, one question at a time, and pick up on tone, hesitation, and the main concern.
Useful questions:
- "So far, what's been [name]'s biggest challenge learning English, Mom?"
- "Is the difficulty more with their English ability, confidence, or staying consistent?"
- "Have they had a learning experience before that didn't quite fit?"
Dig deeper: Pick one main barrier. Ask for a brief example so the concern doesn't stay abstract.

### Point 02 (Biggest Challenge) — Initial Chat

Strategy: Send short messages, 1–2 questions at a time, and use the customer's own words to shape your follow-up.
Useful questions:
- "So far, what's the part that makes [name] struggle most, Mom?"
- "Is it more about confidence, understanding English, practice, or consistency?"
- "Have they learned somewhere else before? Any experience that didn't quite fit?"
Dig deeper: Use the customer's answer as an anchor. One barrier → one follow-up → then move on to the next thing.

## Point 03 — How You Use English: HOW DO YOU CURRENTLY USE ENGLISH?

Need: Understand where, how often, and how the student encounters English in everyday life.
Why it matters: Exposure isn't just "has learned English before." The depth of exposure helps the advisor see whether the student only receives input, or also understands and uses English.
Signals to look for: Frequency of exposure, type of activity, use of subtitles, whether the student understands the context, and whether English is used actively.
What to avoid: Don't infer ability from the amount of exposure alone. Don't assume "watches a lot of English" automatically means a high level.

### Point 03 (How You Use English) — Initial Walk-In

Strategy: Build rapport, observe the customer's and student's reactions, then draw out the answers that surface naturally.
Useful questions:
- "In daily life, where does [name] come across English most often?"
- "When watching YouTube or English films, do they usually use subtitles?"
- "At home or with friends, do they ever use English too?"
Dig deeper: If the customer says "watches a lot of English on YouTube," dig into the depth: do they understand without subtitles or not, do they only watch or also speak, and what topics do they usually watch.

### Point 03 (How You Use English) — Initial Phone Call

Strategy: Keep the conversation light, one question at a time, and pick up on tone, hesitation, and the main concern.
Useful questions:
- "In daily life, where is [name] exposed to English most often?"
- "When watching English content, do they understand on their own or still need subtitles?"
- "Besides school, are there other activities that use a lot of English?"
Dig deeper: Take one example of exposure the customer mentions, then check its quality without letting the conversation run too long.

### Point 03 (How You Use English) — Initial Chat

Strategy: Send short messages, 1–2 questions at a time, and use the customer's own words to shape your follow-up.
Useful questions:
- "In daily life, where does [name] come across English most often, Mom?"
- "For English YouTube/films, do they usually still use subtitles?"
- "Besides school, is there exposure from games, social media, or chatting too?"
Dig deeper: Don't send a long list. Pick the single most relevant source of exposure and ask about its depth.

## Point 04 — How You Like to Learn: HOW DO YOU LIKE TO LEARN?

Need: Understand the way of learning that's most comfortable and effective for the student: the types of activities they enjoy, how they take feedback, how much interaction they need, and the learning environment that helps them stay consistent.
Why it matters: Learning preference helps the advisor choose a more relevant learning journey and classroom approach. The aim isn't to label a learning style, but to understand the conditions that make the student more engaged.
Signals to look for: Preferred activities, individual/group preference, speaking practice, visual/audio/activity-based learning, need for feedback, comfort level with the teacher, and past learning experiences.
What to avoid: Don't force a single 'learning style' as a permanent label. Don't assume the student definitely likes or dislikes a method without exploring why.

### Point 04 (How You Like to Learn) — Initial Walk-In

Strategy: Ask about past learning experiences and the classroom activities that make the student enjoy learning, feel comfortable, and want to take part.
Useful questions:
- When you learn English, what kind of activity do you like most?
- Are you more comfortable learning alone, in pairs, or in a group?
- What part of class usually makes you the most excited?
Dig deeper: Explore why they enjoy that approach and connect it to their learning needs.

### Point 04 (How You Like to Learn) — Initial Phone Call

Strategy: Explore past learning experiences and the learning preferences that make the student more comfortable, engaged, and consistent.
Useful questions:
- When learning English, what kind of activity do you usually prefer?
- Are you more comfortable learning alone or with friends?
- What usually makes learning English feel enjoyable, or instead boring?
Dig deeper: Explore the reasons behind that preference and check whether a past learning experience shaped it.

### Point 04 (How You Like to Learn) — Initial Chat

Strategy: Use chat to learn about the student's preferred activities, past experiences, and the conditions that make them more comfortable learning.
Useful questions:
- When learning English, do you prefer speaking, games, discussion, practice, or other activities?
- Are you more comfortable learning alone or with friends?
- What usually makes you enjoy learning more?
Dig deeper: Don't stop at the choice of activity. Explore the reasons and experiences that gave rise to that preference.

## Point 05 — English Journey: YOUR ENGLISH JOURNEY — CEFR LEVELS

Need: Place the student at a suitable starting point based on their current ability and placement results where available.
Why it matters: Placement isn't just a number. The result helps answer: WHERE ARE YOU NOW? → WHERE DO YOU WANT TO GO? → WHAT LEARNING JOURNEY CAN HELP YOU GET THERE?
Signals to look for: Current level, gap against the goal, the student's response to the placement result, and questions about the starting point or target.
What to avoid: Don't treat placement as a label of ability. Don't add levels/claims that aren't in the official material.

### Point 05 (English Journey) — Initial Walk-In

Strategy: Build rapport, observe the customer's and student's reactions, then draw out the answers that surface naturally.
Useful questions:
- "From this placement result, let me help explain [name]'s starting point, Mom."
- "This part shows their current ability. What's the next target you'd like to reach?"
- "May I show you the CEFR so the level is easier to picture?"
Dig deeper: Use the CEFR Guide to make the level easier to understand. Connect the current level to the goal, not just show a score.

### Point 05 (English Journey) — Initial Phone Call

Strategy: Keep the conversation light, one question at a time, and pick up on tone, hesitation, and the main concern.
Useful questions:
- "Let me help read the placement result as a starting point, Mom."
- "Now that we know the current position, what ability are you aiming for?"
- "I can briefly explain the CEFR so it's easier to picture."
Dig deeper: Use simple, brief language. Make sure the customer understands what the starting point means before moving on to the next part of the journey.

### Point 05 (English Journey) — Initial Chat

Strategy: Send short messages, 1–2 questions at a time, and use the customer's own words to shape your follow-up.
Useful questions:
- "Let me help explain the placement result as [name]'s starting point, Mom."
- "From the current level, what's the most desired next target?"
- "If you'd like, Mom, I can help explain the CEFR so it's easier to picture."
Dig deeper: Explain in 2–3 short sentences. If the customer is confused, send the explanation in steps rather than one long paragraph.

## Point 06 — Success Target Timeline: YOUR SUCCESS TARGET TIMELINE

Need: Determine when the student wants to see results or reach a specific English target, then understand how urgent that target is.
Why it matters: A timeline makes the success target more concrete and helps the advisor judge whether the target, starting point, learning journey, frequency, and schedule are realistic.
Signals to look for: School deadlines, exams, school/university entry, competitions, travel, work/business needs, personal milestones, parent expectations, or a target without a deadline that still needs to be made more specific.
What to avoid: Don't promise results within a certain time without considering the current level, target, consistency, frequency, and learning journey.

### Point 06 (Success Target Timeline) — Initial Walk-In

Strategy: Once the target is clear, ask when the student wants to see results or reach that milestone.
Useful questions:
- When do you want to start seeing noticeable progress?
- Is there a deadline or particular moment that makes this target important?
- When we set the target, what do you want to be able to do by that time?
Dig deeper: Turn an answer of 'as soon as possible' into a more concrete and realistic milestone.

### Point 06 (Success Target Timeline) — Initial Phone Call

Strategy: Explore the urgency and deadline behind the target so the advisor can see a realistic learning timeline.
Useful questions:
- When do you want to start feeling progress?
- Is there an event, exam, school, university, travel, or work need that sets a deadline?
- By that time, what do you want your English ability to look like?
Dig deeper: If there's no deadline, help the customer set a milestone that makes sense.

### Point 06 (Success Target Timeline) — Initial Chat

Strategy: Turn the customer's target into a concrete timeline by asking about the deadline, milestone, and urgency.
Useful questions:
- When do you want to reach this target?
- Is there a deadline or particular moment that makes it important?
- By that time, what do you want to be able to do in English?
Dig deeper: If the answer is still general, help turn it into a milestone that can be pictured.

## Point 07 — Possible Schedule: POSSIBLE SCHEDULE

Need: Find a realistic learning schedule, not just a slot that is theoretically free.
Why it matters: A schedule that fits the student's and family's routine raises the chance the program can actually be carried out consistently.
Signals to look for: Consistently free days/times, routine activities, travel time, parent availability, and possible conflicts.
What to avoid: Don't just ask "when are you free?". Don't offer a schedule before understanding the routine.

### Point 07 (Possible Schedule) — Initial Walk-In

Strategy: Build rapport, observe the customer's and student's reactions, then draw out the answers that surface naturally.
Useful questions:
- "Looking at [name]'s routine, which day is usually the most realistic for learning?"
- "After school, are there tutoring sessions or other activities?"
- "For the time, is late afternoon or evening more comfortable, Mom?"
Dig deeper: Match availability to the real routine: school, work, activities, travel, and parent availability. Avoid assuming a "free slot" automatically means they're ready to learn.

### Point 07 (Possible Schedule) — Initial Phone Call

Strategy: Keep the conversation light, one question at a time, and pick up on tone, hesitation, and the main concern.
Useful questions:
- "So I can find a realistic option, what's [name]'s routine usually like, Mom?"
- "Which day is usually the most open after school/work?"
- "For study time, is late afternoon or evening more comfortable?"
Dig deeper: Ask about the day first, then the time. Once you have options, narrow down to the most realistic schedule.

### Point 07 (Possible Schedule) — Initial Chat

Strategy: Send short messages, 1–2 questions at a time, and use the customer's own words to shape your follow-up.
Useful questions:
- "So the schedule is realistic later, which days is [name] usually available, Mom?"
- "Are there other tutoring sessions/activities after school?"
- "Is late afternoon or evening more comfortable for learning?"
Dig deeper: Ask 1–2 details per message. Once the customer gives a day, move on to the possible time.

## Point 08 — Consultation Notes: CONSULTATION NOTES

Need: Record important information that comes up during the consultation but isn't covered in points 01–07.
Why it matters: Not every important insight can be predicted through prepared questions. Free-form notes help sales capture context that's useful for analysis, recommendations, and follow-up.
Signals to look for: Buying signals, objections, decision maker, urgency, preferences, concerns, observations, spontaneous responses, special circumstances, or other relevant details.
What to avoid: Don't make up information or fill the field just to complete it. Focus on facts, observations, and insights that are genuinely useful.

### Point 08 (Consultation Notes) — Initial Walk-In

Strategy: After the consultation ends, write down any extra insight that came from the conversation or direct observation but wasn't captured in the earlier points.
Useful questions:
- What important detail didn't get asked about?
- What did you observe from the student/customer?
- What insight do you think will be useful for follow-up?
Dig deeper: There's no need to follow the question format. Use this space to note anything you think is important.

### Point 08 (Consultation Notes) — Initial Phone Call

Strategy: After the call, write down important details or insights not captured in the earlier points, including tone, concerns, urgency, and next-step signals.
Useful questions:
- What important detail didn't get asked about?
- What concern or signal came through in the customer's tone?
- What information will be useful for the next follow-up?
Dig deeper: There's no need to follow the question format. Note the relevant facts and insights from the conversation.

### Point 08 (Consultation Notes) — Initial Chat

Strategy: Use this section to summarize important details from the chat not captured in the earlier points, including wording, concerns, buying signals, and context.
Useful questions:
- What important detail didn't get asked about?
- What customer wording or response should be noted?
- What insight will help the next follow-up?
Dig deeper: There's no need to follow the question format. Copy or summarize the important information from the chat concisely and factually.
